# Photo consent — the shooting protocol

> **General information, not legal advice.** It is written to be followed on a phone in a market aisle, not to be filed in court. Anything with real money or a real complaint attached goes to `legal-compliance` first.

The one-line version: **shoot places, not faces.** A photo with no identifiable person in it carries no consent problem, no PIPL problem, and no portrait-rights problem. That is 9 of the 12 launch photos by design (`company/prd/gallery.md` §3). Spend the effort on the other 3.

---

## The card (keep this on your phone)

**Before you press the shutter, one question: is anyone's face recognisable?**

**No →** shoot freely. Tag it `people: 'none'`. Done.

**Yes, and it's a guide or a friend →** get consent (script below), get it **in writing on WeChat**, log it, then shoot.

**Yes, and it's a stranger →** don't. Change the composition instead: shoot from behind, wait for them to leave, crop below the chin, use motion blur, or step back until faces are mush. A Huaqiangbei aisle is just as good a photo without a recognisable face in it.

**Never, under any circumstances:**

- **Anyone who might be under 18.** No exceptions, not even with a parent standing there. Under-14s are sensitive personal data requiring guardian consent, and the reputational downside of "foreign-run app photographs Chinese children" does not have an upside to trade against it.
- **Border and government facilities.** Shenzhen Bay / Luohu / Futian checkpoints, customs and immigration halls, police stations, armed-police posts, military vehicles or personnel, government compounds.
- **Anywhere with a 禁止拍照 sign**, metro and airport security screening, hospitals and clinics, schools and kindergartens, religious sites, and anything political — protests, police activity, slogans, flags.
- **Stall interiors in Huaqiangbei without asking the stallholder.** Many prohibit photography over counterfeit and IP concerns. Ask; don't photograph their face or price board.
- **Drones.** Much of Shenzhen is restricted airspace near the airport and the HK border.

## The consent script

Say it out loud, then **send the same thing on WeChat** so a record exists. The spoken version gets you the yes; the WeChat message is the record.

**中文:**

> 你好，我是〔名字〕，我在做一个叫 Shenzhen Buddies 的网站，帮外国游客认识深圳本地朋友。我刚拍了这张照片，能不能把它放在我们的网站和 Instagram 上？网站服务器在国外，所以照片会传到中国境外，而且是商业用途。你随时可以让我删掉——发微信给我，或者发邮件到〔邮箱〕。可以吗？

**English:**

> I'm 〔name〕, I run a site called Shenzhen Buddies that helps foreign travellers meet locals here. I just took this photo — may I put it on our website and Instagram? The servers are outside China, so the photo leaves the country, and the use is commercial. You can ask me to delete it any time, by WeChat or at 〔email〕. Is that OK?

Six sentences, and each one is load-bearing: who you are, what it's for, **where it will appear**, **that it leaves mainland China**, **that it's commercial**, and **how to withdraw**. Dropping any of them is what turns a yes into an unusable yes.

### The written version — this is the one that counts

The spoken script above gets you the yes. **This is what you send on WeChat**, and it is the actual record. It is longer because PIPL Art. 39 enumerates what an outbound transfer has to disclose, and the short version misses four of them: the named overseas recipient, the categories of information, the full set of rights (not just withdrawal), and how long you keep it.

> ⚠️ **Fill in your registered entity before first use.** `Tensai Tech Inc.` is the operating company, but its **registered jurisdiction is not recorded anywhere in this repo** — do not guess it. Art. 39 asks for the recipient's real name and contact details, and an invented registration is worse than none.

**English:**

> I'm 〔name〕. I run Shenzhen Buddies, a website that helps foreign travellers meet locals in Shenzhen. It's operated by **Tensai Tech Inc.〔, registered in ______ — your actual registered jurisdiction〕** — you can reach me any time at 〔email〕 or on WeChat 〔ID〕.
>
> I'd like to use this photo 〔send the actual photo〕 on our website and on our Instagram, TikTok and YouTube accounts, to show travellers the real people behind the service. This is a commercial use. **Each photo also gets its own link and its own preview card, so this photo can be passed around on its own, away from the rest of the gallery.**
>
> What this means for your information: the photo shows **your face**, and 〔your first name only / no name at all〕. Our website is hosted **outside mainland China — by Vercel Inc. and Supabase Inc., both in the United States** — so the photo will be stored on and shown from servers outside China. If it goes on social media, **Meta, ByteDance and Google** receive it too. We need the photo for this because a marketplace of strangers only works if travellers can see real people; the risk to you is that a photo on the public internet can be copied or saved by others — including the preview card, which the app someone shares it in may keep in its own cache even after we delete ours — and we cannot fully undo that. We keep it until you ask us to remove it, or until we stop using it — whichever comes first.
>
> At any time you can ask me to show you what we hold about you, correct it, or delete it — including the copies held overseas. Just message me or email 〔email〕 and I'll do it and confirm back to you.
>
> **This is completely optional and separate from everything else. If you say no, or say yes now and change your mind later, nothing changes about your work as a guide with us or what you earn.**
>
> Can I use this photo? (Please reply "yes, you can use this photo" so I have a record.)

**中文:**

> 你好，我是〔名字〕，运营 Shenzhen Buddies 这个网站，帮外国游客认识深圳本地朋友。网站由 **Tensai Tech Inc.〔注册地：______〕** 运营，你随时可以通过邮箱〔email〕或微信〔ID〕联系我。
>
> 我想把这张照片〔发送照片本身〕用在我们的网站以及 Instagram、TikTok、YouTube 账号上，让游客看到服务背后真实的人。这属于**商业用途**。**每张照片还有各自的链接和预览卡片，所以这张照片可以脱离整个相册被单独转发。**
>
> 涉及你的哪些信息：这张照片包含**你的面部形象**，以及〔你的名字／不包含姓名〕。我们的网站服务器在**中国大陆境外**，由美国的 **Vercel Inc.** 和 **Supabase Inc.** 提供，所以照片会存储在境外并从境外展示。如果发布到社交平台，**Meta、字节跳动、Google** 也会接收到这张照片。之所以需要这张照片，是因为陌生人之间的平台需要让游客看到真实的人才能建立信任；对你的影响是，公开互联网上的照片可能被他人复制或保存——包括预览卡片，转发所在的应用可能在我们删除之后仍然保留缓存——这一点我们无法完全撤回。我们会保留到你要求删除，或我们不再使用为止，以先发生者为准。
>
> 你随时可以要求我提供、更正或删除这些信息（**包括境外的副本**），微信或邮件〔email〕告诉我即可，我会处理并回复确认。
>
> **这件事完全自愿，并且和其他事情是分开的。你拒绝，或者现在同意以后改主意，都不会影响你在我们这里做向导的工作和收入。**
>
> 可以用这张照片吗？（麻烦回复"同意使用这张照片"，我留个记录。）

**The sentence in bold is the one to never drop.** A guide being asked in person, by the platform that pays them, while you hold a camera, is a textbook power imbalance. PIPL Art. 16 bars refusing service over a declined consent that isn't necessary for that service, and consent given under felt pressure is both legally weak and exactly the situation that produces the post you cannot afford.

Two rules on top:

- **Send the actual photo with the message.** Consent to "a photo" is weaker than consent to *that* photo, and it costs you one tap.
- **For guides, this is a separate message from the guide agreement.** Not a clause in it, not a tickbox bundled with onboarding. Chinese law requires consent for this kind of data to be given separately, and a photo release buried in a contract is the exact thing that fails.

## The record

**A filename is not a consent record.** `guide-lin-laughing-consented.jpg` records only that you typed the word "consented" — not what the person was told, not what they agreed to, not when, and not how to reach them if they change their mind. It also puts a real person's given name into a public repository — and it used to reach the page source of the live site, until `consentRef` was walled off from the browser.

So the record is a **private ledger**, not the repo. Per person, keep:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Consent ID | `SBC-2026-08-001` |
| Date | 2026-08-11 |
| Who | name + WeChat ID (**private ledger only, never the repo**) |
| Photos covered | the actual image files |
| Evidence | screenshot of the WeChat exchange, showing your message and their yes |
| Scope | website + Instagram/TikTok/YouTube, commercial, overseas hosting |
| Credit | how they agreed to be named, or `no credit` — see below |
| Withdrawn? | blank, or the date |

**Allocate the ID by writing the row.** IDs run in sequence (`…-001`, `…-002`), so the only way to get the next one is to open the ledger — which is where the row gets created. Never invent an ID that looks right and backfill the record later; that's the one failure the build check cannot catch, and making ID allocation and record creation the same physical act is what prevents it.

**Credit is a publication decision, not a courtesy.** The `credit` field exists to name a photographer, so putting someone's real name in it publishes their real name. It is currently blocked from reaching the browser, and it should stay that way until someone decides otherwise deliberately. If a guide ever contributes a photo, add one line to the script — *"and can I credit you as 〔what〕?"* — take a first name or a handle, never a full name, and log the answer. At launch the founder shoots everything, so the honest value is the company name or nothing at all.

**Where it lives:** a private folder outside this repository — Google Drive, iCloud, 1Password, anything that isn't git. One subfolder per consent ID. The repo is public-ish and git history is permanent: a personal detail committed by accident cannot be un-published, only re-published with a deletion commit attached to it.

**What goes in the repo:** the consent ID alone. `consentRef: 'SBC-2026-08-001'` — an opaque token that means nothing to anyone without the private ledger. Never a name, never a WeChat ID, never a phone number.

**If someone withdraws:** date the ledger row, delete the item from `src/content/gallery.ts`, delete the WebP from `public/gallery/`, and pull the photo from any social post it appeared in. Chinese law requires withdrawal to be as easy as consent was, so do it and don't negotiate — the parts we control take about ten minutes.

**But be honest that it is not a clean undo, and say so *before* they agree, not after.** Every photo has its own link and preview card, so once one has been shared, the receiving platform has scraped and cached that card, and copies sit in other people's chat histories. We can delete our copy and ask a platform to re-scrape; we cannot reach into WeChat threads or a cache we don't own. That limit is why the script promises "we cannot fully undo that" rather than "we'll delete it" — a withdrawal request we answer with a surprise is worse than the photo ever was. If someone seems hesitant, that is the moment to not take the photo.

## Before the first `people: 'consented'` item ships

**One thing, and it is yours:** the ledger row exists *before* the photo enters `gallery.ts`. Not the same afternoon, not "I'll write it up later" — before.

That is the only step left, because the three code-side holes are now closed and enforced rather than remembered:

- `consentRef` must match `SBC-YYYY-MM-NNN` or `npm run build` fails, so a source filename cannot be pasted in.
- `consentRef` and `credit` are stripped before anything reaches the browser, by an allowlist that makes new fields private by default.
- `marketing/assets/` is gitignored except for `.md` files, so camera originals with their EXIF GPS cannot be committed even by accident.

Which is exactly why the ledger row is the one that needs your discipline: it is the only link in the chain a machine cannot check. A build passes on a well-formed ID whether or not a real person ever said yes.

## Before a batch goes into `gallery.ts`: check the backgrounds

Five minutes at a proper screen, once per batch. **Open each photo at full size and look past the subject.** You shot the noodles; the question is who is sitting behind the noodles.

`people: 'none'` is a claim about every face in the frame, not just the one you were pointing at. Nothing in the build can check it — this is the one gap the type system provably cannot close, so it is the one place a human has to look.

Concentrate on the venue shots, because that is where the risk actually sits. A skyline or a desk of dev boards is nobody; a counter, a stall, a canteen or a shop floor comes with staff and customers as standard. If a recognisable face is in the background of a photo you want: recrop it out, blur it, or drop the photo. There are always more photos.

If you cannot tell whether a face is recognisable, it is. Drop it.

## Why this is stricter than you'd expect elsewhere

Two separate laws attach to one photo in China, and knowing which is which stops you over- or under-reacting:

- **PIPL** treats a recognisable face as *sensitive* personal information, and publishing it requires **separate, specific consent**. Unlike GDPR there is no "legitimate interests" basis to fall back on — consent is effectively the only route. PIPL also treats publishing to overseas servers as a cross-border transfer, which is why the script says the photo leaves China.
- **Civil Code Art. 1019** (portrait rights, 肖像权) is a separate personality right: using someone's likeness without consent is a civil wrong on its own, independent of any data-protection question. This is the one people actually sue over.

The exposure isn't really the damages — Chinese portrait cases settle small. It's that this company's entire product is *strangers trusting us enough to meet in person*. One "they published my face without asking" post does more damage to that than a hundred good photos repair. Which is the whole argument for shooting places instead of faces.
